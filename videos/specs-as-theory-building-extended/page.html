Specs as Theory Building

A walk through Peter Naur's essay, section by section, and what it means for anyone writing specs for coding agents: the theory is the product, and loops that write back governed facts make it compound.

What Naur argued, section by section

  1. 1

    Programming is building a theory

    Two views: in the production view, programming produces a program and other texts; in the theory building view, programmers form an insight, a theory, of the matters at hand, and every document is secondary. Programming matches part of a real-world activity to what a computer can do with symbols, and because the world changes, it includes modifying the program. §1–2, p. 253

  2. 2

    The evidence: two cases

    A second group extended a compiler with full documentation, annotated code and advice from its authors, yet several of their proposals were patches that would have destroyed its power and simplicity; ten years later the structure was still visible but made ineffective by amorphous additions. A 200,000-line monitoring system was kept healthy by the programmers who had been there since its design, while teams with full manuals kept getting stuck. §2, p. 254

  3. 3

    What a theory is (Ryle)

    Intelligent behaviour is doing things well and correcting your lapses, not following rules, since rules for applying rules would regress forever. Having a theory goes further: you can explain, answer questions and argue. It rests on seeing similarities, like applying Newton's laws to pendulums and planets, and such similarity can't be reduced to rules, any more than the likeness of faces or tunes. §3, p. 255

  4. 4

    What the theory makes possible

    Its holder can explain how each part of the program maps to the world, including which parts of the world matter; justify each part; and meet a request for change by seeing how it resembles what is already there. No document can do these. §4, pp. 255–256

  5. 5

    Change and decay

    The hope for cheap modification assumes text editing is the main cost, and Naur calls that argument false. A change can be made many ways, all correct, but only someone with the theory can tell which extend it and which are patches. That is how programs decay. §5, pp. 256–258

  6. 6

    Life, death and revival

    A program lives while a team holding its theory controls its changes. It dies when that team dissolves, even if it keeps running, and rebuilding the theory from code and documents alone is "strictly impossible". Theory passes on the way music is taught: by working closely with people who have it. §6, pp. 258–259

  7. 7

    Method and the programmer

    A method is a set of work rules, but a theory has no inherent parts or order, so "there can be no right method"; methods help as education. Programmers are responsible, permanent developers of the activity the computer is part of, with the standing of engineers and lawyers. §7–8, pp. 259–261

Loops that compound

If every spec starts from scratch, every team rediscovers the same rules, platform quirks and owners, and longer documents don't fix that. A loop does. The loop is this cut's extension of Naur, not his claim.

  1. Discoverwhat's true: rules, systems, people
  2. Verifywith the people who own each fact
  3. Specifythe handoff, built on those facts
  4. Buildwith agents
  5. Learnfrom what building reveals
  6. Write backwhat you learned, as governed facts

A governed fact cites its source, names an owner who vouches for it, carries a date, and says whether it is verified or still assumed. Decisions keep their reasons and the options they rejected.

The next loop starts from those facts. An adjacent feature inherits most of them; an unrelated one still reuses the rules, platforms and people they share. Each loop has fewer unknowns to resolve, so it moves faster, and what's left is the work that needs judgment. When a rule changes, you can see every spec that relied on it.

This works with Naur, not around him. Facts aren't the theory, but they're what it's built on, and the loop keeps the people who hold the theory in it, verifying and deciding. That is what keeps a product alive.

Five habits for PMs writing specs

  1. Trace every requirement to a governed fact

    Naur: map

    Source, owner, date, and whether it's verified or assumed. Naur: deciding which parts of the world are relevant "can only be made by someone who understands the whole world."

  2. Write down the why, and what you rejected

    Naur: justify

    An agent can implement a list of requirements. It can't infer the reasons behind them.

  3. Rehearse change

    Naur: adapt

    Run likely changes through the spec. If a new rule fits the design, the theory is sound; if it needs a patch, the theory has a gap.

  4. Close each loop by writing back what you learned

    Compounds

    What one loop verifies is where the next one starts, so each spec begins with fewer unknowns.

  5. Keep the theory's holders in the loop as agents build

    Keeps it alive

    Facts help people build the theory; they don't replace the people. A program nobody can change intelligently is already dead.

<loop>        ::= <discover> <verify> <specify> <build> <learn> <write-back>
<spec>        ::= <requirement> { <requirement> } <change-rehearsal>
<requirement> ::= <what> <why> <fact> { <fact> } { <rejected-option> }
<fact>        ::= <claim> <source> <owner> <date> ( "verified" | "assumed" )
<write-back>  ::= { <fact> | <decision> }
<decision>    ::= <choice> <why> { <rejected-option> }

The loop and the habits as a grammar, in Backus–Naur Form, the notation that carries Naur's name.

Naur, in his own words

"The proper, primary aim of programming is, not to produce programs, but to have the programmers build theories of the manner in which the problems at hand are solved by program execution."

Abstract, p. 253

"A person who has or possesses a theory in this sense knows how to do certain things and in addition can support the actual doing with explanations, justifications, and answers to queries, about the activity of concern."

§3, on Ryle's notion of theory, p. 255

"The theory built by the programmers has primacy over such other products as program texts, user documentation, and additional documentation such as specifications."

§4, pp. 255–256

"For this support to be valid it must clearly be assumed that the dominating cost is one of text manipulation. … On the Theory Building View this whole argument is false."

§5, on the cost of modifications, p. 257

"The death of a program happens when the programmer team possessing its theory is dissolved. A dead program may continue to be used for execution in a computer and to produce useful results."

§6, p. 258

"A very important consequence of the Theory Building View is that program revival, that is re-establishing the theory of a program merely from the documentation, is strictly impossible."

§6, p. 258

"It follows that on the Theory Building View, for the primary activity of the programming there can be no right method."

§7, p. 259

Peter Naur, "Programming as Theory Building," Microprocessing and Microprogramming 15 (1985): 253–261
